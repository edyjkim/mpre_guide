Universal heuristics

These cross-cutting rules apply to every question and hold across outline subsections. Each states a default, then every exception, so no lookup is needed.

RuleExceptions
1

The client makes the big calls (settle, plea, jury waiver, testify, appeal); the lawyer picks tactics after consulting (MR 1.2(a); Restatement § 22).

Except where professional duties override the client

  • No assisting crime or fraudMR 1.2(d)
  • Candor to the tribunalMR 3.3
  • Refusing evidence the lawyer reasonably believes is false, except a criminal defendant's own testimonyMR 3.3(a)(3)

ExampleA client rejects a settlement offer the lawyer likes, and the lawyer must follow that decision while still choosing which witnesses to call.

2

Never act for the lawyer's fees, convenience or reputation at the client's expense.

Except where a rule allows it

  • Revealing confidences to collect a fee or defend against an accusationMR 1.6(b)(5)
  • Withdrawing for nonpayment after warning, or for unreasonable financial burdenMR 1.16(b)(5)–(6)
  • A lien to secure feesMR 1.8(i)(1)

ExampleA lawyer can't stall a closing to bill more hours, but may reveal confidences needed to sue for an unpaid fee.

3

A "must" rule needs actual knowledge; a "may" rule needs only reasonable belief (MR 1.0(f), (i)).

Except duties triggered by what the lawyer reasonably should know

  • Trial publicityMR 3.6
  • Correcting an unrepresented person's misunderstandingMR 4.3
  • Notifying the sender of inadvertently sent documentsMR 4.4(b)
  • Harassment or discriminationMR 8.4(g)
  • Telling an organization's constituent that the lawyer represents the organization when their interests are adverseMR 1.13(f)
  • A neutral explaining the neutral's role to a party who misunderstands itMR 2.4(b)

ExampleA lawyer who merely suspects a civil witness will lie has no duty to act, but may refuse to call the witness if the belief is reasonable.

4

Confidentiality exceptions and reporting out are "may" rules, never "must" (MR 1.6(b), 1.13(c)).

Except where disclosure is compelled

  • To the tribunal, to remedy false evidence or fraud on the proceeding, overriding 1.6MR 3.3(c)
  • To the tribunal in an ex parte proceeding, all material facts, even adverse onesMR 3.3(d)
  • By prosecutors, of exculpatory evidenceMR 3.8(d)
  • To the client, of what they need to decideMR 1.4
  • To a third person, of a material fact when needed to avoid assisting the client's crime or fraud, but only if 1.6 allows the disclosureMR 4.1(b)

ExampleA client says he intends to seriously injure a coworker, so the lawyer may disclose to prevent it but isn't required to.

5

Most conflicts are fine once each affected client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing (MR 1.7(b), 1.9(a)).

Except current-client conflicts consent can't cure (MR 1.7(b))

  • The lawyer can't reasonably believe they can represent each client competently and diligently
  • The law prohibits the representation
  • One client asserts a claim against another in the same litigation

Except arrangements consent never cures

  • Soliciting a substantial gift, or drafting an instrument giving one to the lawyer or a relative, unless relatedMR 1.8(c)
  • Media rights before the matter endsMR 1.8(d)
  • Financial help beyond court costs, litigation expenses and pro bono living-expense giftsMR 1.8(e)
  • A stake in the case beyond liens and contingent feesMR 1.8(i)
  • Sexual relations with a current client, unless the relationship predates the representationMR 1.8(j)
  • A contingent fee in criminal defense, or one tied to securing a divorce or the amount of alimony, support or a property settlementMR 1.5(d)

ExampleTwo co-plaintiffs whose interests could diverge may share a lawyer once each gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.

6

Oral consent plus the lawyer's prompt written confirmation is enough (MR 1.0(b), 1.7(b)(4)).

Except

  • A writing signed by the client is required for contingent fees (1.5(c)), business transactions with clients (1.8(a)) and aggregate settlements (1.8(g))
  • No writing is required for limited scope (1.2(c)), disclosure (1.6(a)) or third-party payment (1.8(f))

ExampleThe client agrees to a conflict by phone and the lawyer emails a confirmation, which is enough without any signature.

7

A timely screen plus notice keeps one lawyer's conflict from disqualifying the firm (MR 1.10(a)(2), 1.11(b), 1.12(c), 1.18(d)).

Except where no screen works

  • The screened lawyer's own disqualification
  • A current-client conflict under 1.7
  • The 1.8 conflicts imputed by 1.8(k)

ExampleA lawyer joins a firm opposing her former client in the same matter, and a timely screen, no fee share and written notice keep the firm in.

8

After the matter ends, confidentiality and the duty to return property and unearned fees continue (MR 1.9(c), 1.16(d)).

Except

  • Loyalty shrinks from no direct adversity at all (1.7) to not in the same or a substantially related matter (1.9(a)), so suing a former client on an unrelated matter is proper

ExampleA lawyer may sue a former client on an unrelated contract but can't use its confidential information.

9

Using an agent or the client to do it is still the lawyer's violation (MR 8.4(a), 5.3).

Except

  • Represented parties may talk to each other, and the lawyer may advise the client about itMR 4.2 cmt. [4]
  • Nonlawyer staff conflicts aren't imputed when screenedMR 1.10 cmt. [4]
  • The lawyer answers for a nonlawyer's misconduct only if the lawyer ordered or ratified it, or, as a partner, manager or direct supervisor, knew in time to avoid or mitigate it and failed to act (5.3(c)); the duty to make reasonable efforts to supervise (5.3(a)–(b)) applies regardless

ExampleA lawyer who has an investigator call a represented opposing party violates MR 4.2 as if he had made the call himself.

Regulation of the legal profession

Discipline reaches any conduct bearing on honesty or fitness, wherever it happens, and supervisors answer for what they order, ratify, or fail to fix.

The client-lawyer relationship

The client owns the ends and the big decisions; the lawyer owns the means, must keep the client informed, and must charge a reasonable fee.

Conflicts of interest

Competence, legal malpractice, and other civil liability

An unfamiliar matter is not a violation if the lawyer gets competent or brings in help; liability needs duty, breach, causation and damages, not just a rule violation.

PreviousIV. Conflicts of interestNextVI. Litigation and other forms of advocacy

Litigation and other forms of advocacy

Zealous advocacy stops at the tribunal's integrity. When candor (MR 3.3) and confidentiality (MR 1.6) collide, candor wins.

PreviousV. Competence, legal malpractice, and other civil liabilityNextVII. Transactions and communications with persons other than clients

Transactions and communications with persons other than clients

No lies about material facts, no going around a represented person's lawyer, and no posing as neutral to someone unrepresented.

PreviousVI. Litigation and other forms of advocacyNextVIII. Different roles of the lawyer

Different roles of the lawyer

Identify the role first. A prosecutor's job is justice, an organization's lawyer serves the entity, and a neutral represents no one.

PreviousVII. Transactions and communications with persons other than clientsNextIX. Safekeeping funds and other property

Safekeeping funds and other property

Client money stays separate until earned or owed, disputed money stays in trust, and intent never excuses misuse.

PreviousVIII. Different roles of the lawyerNextX. Communications about legal services

Communications about legal services

Advertising in any medium is fine if it isn't misleading; the danger zone is live, person-to-person contact for money and paying people for referrals.

PreviousIX. Safekeeping funds and other propertyNextXI. Lawyers' duties to the public and the legal system

Lawyers' duties to the public and the legal system

Pro bono is aspirational, appointments are hard to refuse, and lawyers may not buy, fake or misuse influence over officials.

PreviousX. Communications about legal servicesNextXII. Judicial conduct

Judicial conduct

Ask whether a reasonable person would question the judge's impartiality, and whether an off-bench activity trades on the office or will pull the judge into disqualification. The outline has no separate line for campaigns, so political activity is placed under XII.A.
